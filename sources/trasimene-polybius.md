# Polybius on Lake Trasimene (paraphrase of Histories 3.80–3.84)

Source text for `scenarios/trasimene-217bc.json`. **This is a paraphrase written for this
example, not a translation.** It follows Polybius, *Histories* book 3 (public domain in the
original and in the old English translations); section numbers are given so it can be checked.
Livy's account (22.4–7) agrees in outline.

**3.80–82.** The consul Flaminius was encamped at Arretium. Hannibal, judging him rash and
eager for glory, marched past him towards Rome, burning and plundering, to provoke him into
following. Flaminius broke camp and followed, against the advice of his officers.

**3.83.** Hannibal, now near the lake called Trasimene, saw a place well suited to an ambush. The
road ran along a narrow strip of level ground, with a long line of hills on one side and the lake
on the other; at the far end rose a steep hill, hard to climb, and at the near end the way in,
between the hills and the lake, was narrow. He took the hill at the far end with his African and
Spanish infantry and camped there in plain view. He led his Balearic slingers and light troops
round behind the hills on the right and posted them along the slopes, out of sight; and he put
his cavalry and the Gauls behind the hills on the left, stretching back to the narrow entrance, so
that once the Romans were inside they would be shut in by the lake and the hills.

**3.84.** Flaminius reached the lake late in the evening and camped near the entrance. At dawn
he led his column into the valley. A thick mist had risen from the lake and the low ground, while
the hilltops were clear, so that Hannibal could see the whole Roman column and the Romans could
see almost nothing. When the head of the column was close to the hill at the far end, Hannibal
gave the signal, and every part of his army attacked at once from the hills. The Romans, unable
to see where the enemy was or to form up, were cut down where they stood. Flaminius was killed;
about fifteen thousand Romans died in the valley, many of them driven into the lake. About six
thousand at the head of the column broke through, but were surrounded and surrendered the next
day.
