# Herodotus on Thermopylae (paraphrase of Histories 7.175–7.225)

Source text for `scenarios/thermopylae-480bc.json`. **This is a paraphrase written for this
example, not a translation.** It follows Herodotus, *Histories* book 7 (public domain in the
original and in the old English translations); section numbers are given so it can be checked.

**7.175–176.** The Greeks decided to hold the pass at Thermopylae, which was narrower than the
pass into Thessaly. They did not know of the path that later doomed them until they arrived and
learned of it from the people of Trachis. On one side of the road a steep, impassable mountain
rises towards Oeta; on the other lie the sea and marshes. Near Alpeni, behind the pass, and again
near the Phoenix stream and at Anthela, the road is only wide enough for a single cart. Long
before, the Phocians had built a wall with gates across the pass, and it was rebuilt now.

**7.201, 7.210–212.** Xerxes camped near Trachis, the Greeks in the pass. After waiting four
days he sent the Medes in, then his own Immortals. In the narrow space their numbers were no
use; the Spartans repeatedly turned as if in flight, then wheeled round and cut down their
pursuers. Two days of attacks failed.

**7.213–216.** A man of Malis, Ephialtes, told Xerxes of the path over the mountain to
Thermopylae. It starts at the Asopos river where it flows through the gorge, runs along the
ridge of the mountain (the mountain and the path are both called Anopaea), and comes down at
Alpenus, the first town of the Locrians, behind the Greek position.

**7.217–218.** Hydarnes and the Immortals set out at the hour when lamps are lit and marched all
night. At dawn they reached the summit, where a thousand Phocians were posted to guard the path.
The Phocians heard the trampling of leaves in the oak woods, ran to arm, and fell back to the top
of the mountain expecting to be attacked; the Persians ignored them and hurried down.

**7.219–222.** Scouts running down from the heights brought the news at daybreak. Most of the
allies left; Leonidas stayed with the Spartans, the Thespians and the Thebans.

**7.223–225.** In the morning the Greeks went out into the wider part of the pass and fought
there. Leonidas fell. When they learned that the Persians who had come round by the path were
upon them, they fell back into the narrow part behind the wall and took their stand on the
hillock at the entrance, where the stone lion now stands. There they were overwhelmed, attacked
from the front and from behind.
