# Which pass did Hannibal use? (illustrative discussion)

Source text for `scenarios/hannibal-alps-218bc.json`. **This is not a real thread.** It was written
for this example in the style of a forum discussion, to show how a scenario maps a debate; the
usernames are invented. The positions it summarises are real ones from the long-running debate.

---

**alpine_curious:** Polybius says that on the summit Hannibal showed his troops the plains of the
Po to lift their spirits, and that snow was already lying on the pass. Livy adds the rockfall on
the way down that his men broke up with fire and vinegar. Which pass fits? I keep reading Col de
la Traversette, Col du Clapier, Mont Cenis, the Little St Bernard and Montgenèvre.

**geoarch_fan:** Traversette. It's brutal, nearly 3,000 m, but a 2016 study found a layer of
churned-up, dung-rich soil in a mire below the pass on the French side, dated to around 200 BC,
which they read as the passage of a large army with horses. From near the top you look straight
down the upper Po valley.

**clapier_guy:** The height alone is a problem for an army with elephants late in the year. Col
du Clapier is lower, reached up the Maurienne (the Arc valley), and it's the pass people cite most
for a real view onto the Italian side close to the summit. The Italian side is steep, which fits
the rockfall story.

**romanroads:** Why not Montgenèvre or Mont Cenis? They're the easiest crossings, and later the
main roads into Italy. The Little St Bernard was a favourite of 19th-century writers too, up the
Isère and down into the Aosta valley, but that brings you out far to the north, among the
Salassi, a long way from the Taurini, whose town Polybius says Hannibal stormed soon after coming
down.

**alpine_curious:** So the trade-off is the hard, high pass with the physical evidence against the
lower, easier passes that later armies used. Can we at least see what each route would have meant
on the ground?
